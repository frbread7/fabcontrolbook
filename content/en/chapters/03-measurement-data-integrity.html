Measurement Noise, Sampling, and Data Integrity

Learning objectives

Separate process variation from measurement variation, reason about sampling and aliasing, quantify uncertainty in a monitored statistic, and define validation checks that protect downstream SPC and control decisions.

Concepts and measurement model

A measured result can be represented as Y = X + B + ε, where X is the measurand, B is a bias term, and ε is random measurement error. In practice, the observation also depends on tool, method, operator, calibration, environment, and sample preparation. Repeatability describes variation under closely repeated conditions; reproducibility includes changed conditions. A stable gauge can still be biased, and a precise gauge can still be too noisy for a small process shift.

For independent, zero-mean errors with standard deviation σm and independent process observations with standard deviation σp, total observed variance is σy2 = σp2 + σm2. This variance decomposition fails when terms are correlated or when bias drifts. Repeated measurements on one wafer estimate some measurement variability but do not create independent process samples. A measurement-system study should reflect the actual sampling design and relevant factors, not a convenient laboratory repeat.

Sampling interval sets what can be seen. If a sensor samples too slowly relative to a transient, aliasing may make a rapid oscillation appear as a slower or absent pattern. A sampling theorem provides a lower bound for band-limited signals under ideal assumptions, but industrial traces also face filtering, trigger, and clock limitations. For a run-level statistic, define the signal window, excluded startup/shutdown regions, filters, missing-data policy, and feature units. These choices are part of the monitor specification and should be versioned.

Noise changes chart performance. If individual independent measurements have standard deviation σ, the standard error of an average of n observations is σ/√n. That reduction does not apply when samples are positively autocorrelated. For a stationary series with autocorrelation ρk, the variance of the sample mean is approximately σ²/n times [1 + 2Σρk] over relevant lags (with finite-sample weights). Treating correlated readings as independent makes limits too narrow and alarms too frequent. Subgrouping should therefore follow process physics and timing, not an arbitrary row count.

Integrity checks include units and scale, monotonic and plausible timestamps, cadence, duplicate IDs, missingness, range and saturation, calibration state, recipe/context completeness, and transformation version. A check can mark data unsuitable for a particular analysis without asserting that the physical process is faulty. Preserve raw and cleaned data, the rejection reason, and the number of observations affected. Corrections must be traceable; overwriting raw sensor values prevents forensic analysis.

Worked example: duplicate measurements

Suppose 100 unique wafers have sample SD 0.20 units. The independent-unit standard error is 0.20/√100 = 0.020. A replay duplicates every source-event ID, yielding 200 rows but still 100 wafers and 100 independent sampling units; the correct standard error remains 0.020. The naive row-based value 0.20/√200 = 0.0141 is unjustified pseudoreplication. Deduplicate by source-event identity for the estimate, preserve the replayed rows and ingest audit for lineage, and count unique wafers—not rows—as the denominator. If wafers are clustered in lots or adjacent outputs are serially dependent, the effective information can be lower still; model or sample at the independent experimental unit appropriate to the question.

Limitations and decision risks

Variance decomposition is only as useful as its assumptions and experimental design. Unmodeled calibration drift, changing gauges, selection bias, censoring, non-normal tails, and common-mode disturbances can dominate. A control limit built from corrupted data can institutionalize a bad baseline. A statistically significant difference may be operationally irrelevant; a small number of measurements may miss an important excursion. Keep control limits separate from engineering specification limits, and evaluate uncertainty and practical effect size together.
