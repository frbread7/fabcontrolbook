Semiconductor Manufacturing Control Architecture

Learning objectives

After this chapter, you should be able to distinguish monitoring from feedback control, place SPC, FDC, APC, and run-to-run (R2R) control in a manufacturing architecture, and identify the evidence needed before an automated action is allowed.

Concepts and engineering model

A fab control system is a chain of decisions over material, equipment, recipes, measurements, and time. A wafer or lot acquires context as it moves: product and route, operation, tool and chamber, recipe revision, timestamps, and measurement method. That context is part of the observation. A value without it cannot reliably identify which process produced the result.

Statistical Process Control (SPC) compares a defined process statistic with limits estimated from a stable reference period. It signals evidence of a change; it does not by itself identify a cause or command a tool. Fault Detection and Classification (FDC) evaluates equipment or process signals, often within a run, and flags signatures that differ from an accepted operating envelope. Advanced Process Control (APC) selects a manipulated variable to reduce an error or meet a target under a process model. R2R is a sampled-data form: one wafer or lot is processed, measured, and then the next recipe is adjusted. These functions can share data while retaining distinct responsibilities.

A useful abstraction is y[k] = f(u[k], d[k], c[k]) + e[k], where y is an outcome such as thickness in nm, u is an intentional recipe input, d is an unmeasured disturbance, c is context (tool, chamber, product, material, and recipe), and e is measurement error. The model is conceptual: real processes may be nonlinear, time-varying, coupled, and only partially observed. Sensors, metrology, MES/dispatch records, historian systems, and control services must preserve common identity and time semantics before their data can be combined.

Governance should separate four decisions: detect, diagnose, disposition, and control. A monitor may raise an advisory; an engineer checks data quality and context; material disposition follows approved procedures; only a validated controller may change a bounded recipe variable. Interlocks, rate limits, authorization, audit logs, rollback, and a safe fallback are prerequisites for actuation. Control limits describe process behavior; specification limits describe product acceptance. They answer different questions and must not be substituted for one another.

Worked example: thickness drift

All quantities below are synthetic. Assume the stable Phase I reference supports target μ₀ = 100.0 nm and known individual-wafer σ = 0.8 nm, with independent wafer results under one chamber, recipe revision, and measurement method. For the next decision, average n = 5 distinct wafers: the standard error is 0.8/√5 = 0.358 nm and the two-sided 3σ X̄ limits are 100.0 ± 3(0.358), or 98.927 to 101.073 nm. A subgroup mean of 101.1 nm exceeds the upper limit by 0.027 nm. This is a signal against the stated baseline; the small excess does not quantify a defect probability or identify a cause. If σ and the center were estimated rather than treated as fixed, their estimation uncertainty and the selected Phase I design would change the actual signal rate.

Decision: freeze this result out of automatic recipe feedback; verify the five unique wafer IDs, measurement method and calibration, chamber, recipe revision, timestamps, and subgroup rule. Then compare event-aligned chamber traces and maintenance records and obtain an independent metrology check if available. Route material through the site disposition authority. No product specification was supplied, so neither acceptance nor rejection follows from this chart. Only after cause and input-response evidence are reviewed may a separately validated controller propose a bounded move; equipment interlocks and authorized approval remain the actuator gate.

Limitations and operating cautions

Aggregating different products, chambers, recipe versions, or metrology methods can create apparent shifts or mask real ones. Separate strata only when the process and measurement rationale supports the split; excessive stratification reduces data and can hide common causes. Wafer observations may be serially correlated, and lot averages may share chamber or time effects, violating independent-sample assumptions. A chart's false-alarm rate depends on its reference model and sampling scheme. Monitoring is not causal inference, and a controller built from a weakly identified model can amplify disturbances. The architecture must expose missingness, delayed results, rework, censoring, and changes to data definitions rather than silently dropping them.
