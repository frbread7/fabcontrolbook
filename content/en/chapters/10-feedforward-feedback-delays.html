Feedforward, Feedback, Delays, and Disturbances

Learning objectives

Distinguish feedforward from feedback by the information each uses and when that information becomes available. Analyze how transport, sampling, computation, and metrology delay change a monitoring or control decision. Design a layered response that does not confuse an early warning with proof of a wafer outcome.

A fab control loop crosses equipment sensors, context systems, process execution, metrology, data transport, and operator disposition. Its effective delay is the sum of relevant components, but this sum may vary by product, route, queue, and measurement method. Preserve timestamps and run identifiers at each boundary; a numerically correct control law applied to stale or misassociated data is still wrong.

Information timing and loop behavior

With zero initial-state terms, write a local linear time-invariant model in the Laplace domain as Y(s)=G(s)U(s)+Gd(s)D(s). The capital letters are transforms of response, manipulated input, and disturbance; multiplying transfer functions by time-domain u(t) or d(t) would mix domains. G has response/input units, Gd response/disturbance units, and their ratio input/disturbance units. Feedforward uses a measured disturbance before its output effect. Ideal scalar cancellation asks for Uff(s)=−Gd(s)D(s)/G(s), only if the inverse is causal, stable, proper, feasible, and supported by timely disturbance measurement. Those conditions and zero initial states are assumptions, not properties guaranteed by a fab process.

Feedback instead forms an error e(t)=r(t)−y(t) and changes u after observing the response. It can reject unmeasured disturbances, but only after sensing, processing, and actuation latency. A pure delay τ contributes the factor e−sτ; it adds phase lag and reduces stability margin in a closed loop. In sampled systems, identify whether the measurement corresponds to the just-completed run, a prior run, or a moving window. Do not use “latest value” as a substitute for event-time association.

Feedforward and feedback are complementary when their models and data lineage are trustworthy: feedforward can reduce a known disturbance promptly, while feedback corrects residual error. Add a bounded feedback trim only after checking signs, units, delays, interactions, and saturation. If a disturbance sensor fails, feedforward should enter an explicitly defined hold or fallback state; it should not silently substitute a stale signal. If feedback metrology is delayed, updates must be queued against the measured run and controller state.

Worked example: a delayed metrology result

Consider a fictional lot process with output yk=uk+dk in normalized response units. An upstream sensor measures disturbance d with error standard deviation 0.1 units before the lot; a downstream metrology result arrives two lots later. Assume unit process gain, no cross-lot coupling, and a target of zero. A nominal feedforward action is uk=−d̂k. With disturbance estimate 0.4, it selects −0.4, but the sensor uncertainty leaves residual output uncertainty of approximately 0.1 response units under these assumptions.

Assume the result for lot 20 arrives only after lots 21 and 22 have run. The event-time and arrival-time sequence is explicit:

On a narrow screen, swipe the table or focus it and use arrow keys. Keep lot-20 measurement event time, arrival time, recipe revision, chamber, calibration, and controller-state version. The result may update a model under an approved rule but must not be assigned to lot 22. Here two intervening lot decisions lacked lot-20 feedback; a different route or queue could change that count. This is an illustrative accounting result, not an allowable production latency.

Assumptions and safeguards

Delay can vary, disturbance sensors can drift, process gain can change with recipe or chamber, and measurements can be autocorrelated. An inverse feedforward model can amplify noise or demand infeasible input. Feedback cannot recover information that has not yet arrived. Define maximum data age, missing/out-of-order behavior, controller hold conditions, saturation handling, rollback authority, and independent equipment interlocks before automation. Product specifications, statistical control limits, and equipment-safe bounds answer different questions. None of the examples in this chapter is a qualified recipe recommendation or a validated production controller.

Review questions

  2. Why can an inverse process model make feedforward noise-sensitive or nonimplementable?

  4. How should a delayed result be associated when several lots are in flight?
  5. Describe a safe behavior when the disturbance sensor is stale or invalid.
