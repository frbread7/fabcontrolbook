SPC Control Charts and Rational Subgrouping

Learning objectives

Choose a meaningful statistical unit, explain rational subgrouping, distinguish common-cause behavior from evidence of special causes, and state when standard Shewhart limits are not justified.

Concepts and chart construction

A control chart is a time-ordered decision rule for a defined statistic. Its center line represents a reference process; control limits quantify expected variation under a model. They are not engineering tolerances or customer specifications. The chart is useful only when the data's sequence, sampling unit, and reference period correspond to the process question. A limit crossing signals unusual evidence under the baseline model; it does not automatically identify a defect, root cause, or disposition.

Rational subgrouping places observations produced under conditions expected to be similar within a subgroup, so within-subgroup spread estimates short-term variation while subgroup-to-subgroup movement can reveal changes. In a fab, that design is not automatic. Multiple dies on one wafer share process history; wafers in a lot may share chamber conditions; measurements across chambers may confound tool differences with time. Pooling across products, recipes, or chambers can inflate spread and hide shifts; splitting every factor can reduce signal power and create many opportunities for false alarms. State the estimand and physical rationale before charting.

For independent approximately normal individual observations with stable mean μ and standard deviation σ, an individuals chart commonly uses center μ and limits μ ± 3σ. For subgroup means of size n under independent within-subgroup observations, the standard error is σ/√n, giving limits μ ± 3σ/√n. In practice, σ is estimated; estimates and finite sample choices alter the actual false-alarm behavior. For subgroup ranges or standard deviations, constants depend on n and the assumed distribution. Use a recognized derivation or software implementation and document which estimator and phase-I baseline were used.

Phase I evaluates historical data for a defensible stable reference; phase II monitors future observations against frozen limits. Removing points solely because they breach limits biases the baseline and can normalize real faults. Exclusions require assignable-cause evidence and documented rationale. Recalculate limits after a process or measurement-system change only through controlled review; do not widen limits to reduce alarms. Use run rules sparingly and predefine them because multiple rules increase false alarms.

Autocorrelation violates the independence assumptions behind many textbook limits. A slowly drifting but otherwise predictable series may trigger repeated signals even without a new fault; alternatively, smoothing can delay detection. Consider monitoring a justified residual from a time-series model, using methods with dependence-aware limits, or sampling at a process-relevant interval. Validate the chosen approach on representative data and test its operational alarm burden. Never apply an individuals chart to dense sensor points as if each were an independent wafer outcome.

Worked example: a wafer statistic

Phase I synthetic reference: one chamber/recipe, four consecutive wafers per subgroup, with each wafer result treated as independent within and between subgroups for this calculation. Across stable historical data, set center μ₀ = 10.00 nm and a known individual-result σ = 0.20 nm. For n = 4, the X̄ standard error is 0.20/√4 = 0.10 nm and limits are 10.00 ± 3(0.10), or 9.70 and 10.30 nm. Four ordered subgroup means are 10.00, 10.10, 10.15, and 10.45 nm; the fourth signals above the upper limit. The subgrouping is meaningful only if these four measurements represent the intended short-term process window; four adjacent sensor rows would not automatically qualify.

The 10.30 nm boundary is a statistical control limit under the stated baseline assumptions, not a product acceptance threshold. A wafer at 10.45 nm may still meet a product specification, or a value inside 9.70–10.30 may fail one; specification limits were not supplied. Investigate the signal and data integrity first, retain the Phase I exclusions with assignable-cause evidence, and do not widen limits merely to suppress recurrence.

Limitations and assumptions

Three-sigma limits are not universal guarantees: estimated parameters, non-normal data, autocorrelation, nonstationarity, small samples, and repeated testing change false-alarm probabilities. A chart may detect a distributional change but miss an important tail or spatial defect mode. Stratification can improve interpretability but should reflect a causal or measurement distinction, not merely optimize a chart. Control-chart evidence should be combined with engineering context and measurement validation.
