Shewhart, EWMA, and CUSUM Detection

Learning objectives

Compare Shewhart, exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA), and cumulative sum (CUSUM) charts; calculate their core statistics; and explain how shift size, sampling, assumptions, and alarm policy affect delay and false alarms.

Detection theory and equations

All three chart families repeatedly test observations against a reference model. A Shewhart chart signals when a current statistic is far from the baseline. For standardized independent observations z[k] = (x[k] − μ₀)/σ, a two-sided rule may signal at |z[k]| > 3. It is relatively responsive to large abrupt changes but uses little information from preceding samples. A three-sigma limit has a 0.0027 per-point exceedance probability only for known μ₀, σ and independent standard-normal observations; parameter estimation, skew, autocorrelation, and additional run rules alter this rate.

EWMA retains history recursively: w[k] = λz[k] + (1 − λ)w[k−1], with w[0] = 0 for a centered baseline. Smaller λ spreads weight farther into the past and improves sensitivity to some persistent small shifts, at the cost of slower response to abrupt large shifts. For independent unit-variance z values, Var(w[k]) = λ/(2−λ) · [1−(1−λ)^(2k)]. A time-varying limit is ±L√Var(w[k]); the steady-state factor is ±L√(λ/(2−λ)). Here λ and L must be selected for a stated false-alarm and detection objective, not copied without regard to sampling frequency.

A one-sided standardized CUSUM for upward shifts is C⁺[k] = max(0, C⁺[k−1] + z[k] − κ); signal if C⁺[k] > h. The downward counterpart is C⁻[k] = max(0, C⁻[k−1] − z[k] − κ). Reference value κ is often chosen as half a target shift in standard-deviation units for a normal mean-shift design; h determines the alarm threshold. This is a design convention, not a universal optimum. Reset policy, head start, baseline estimation, subgroup size, and correlated data all affect performance. CUSUM accumulates weak evidence and can detect sustained small shifts efficiently, but may be harder to interpret operationally without a clear signal and reset procedure.

Average run length (ARL) is the expected number of plotted points to an alarm under specified conditions: ARL₀ under the in-control model and ARL₁ under a chosen shift. It is not elapsed time unless the sampling cadence is fixed and regular. Compare candidate charts with the same baseline, sampling, and alarm policy. Monte Carlo estimates need enough independent sequences and uncertainty intervals; analytical formulas require their own assumptions. In the finite-horizon labs, probability estimates show 95% Wilson intervals, while delay values are descriptive point summaries conditional on detected trials and have no interval. A single impressive detection example does not characterize long-run false alarms.

A monitoring rule also includes what happens after a signal: suppress duplicate alarms, freeze or reset chart state only under controlled criteria, identify affected material, and route evidence to an accountable response. Raising the threshold to ease alarm burden increases missed shifts or delay. Never translate an alarm into automatic recipe action without a validated controller and safety boundary.

Worked example: subtle mean shift

For independent synthetic standardized observations z = (0.4, 0.6, 0.2, 0.8), initialize w₀ = C⁺₀ = C⁻₀ = 0; use λ = 0.2, κ = 0.25, and illustrative h = 1.5. The EWMA recurrence gives w = (0.080, 0.184, 0.187, 0.310). Its finite-start variance is λ/(2−λ)[1−(1−λ)^(2k)]: at k = 1, 0.04 (SD 0.200), and at k = 4, about 0.0925 (SD 0.304); the limiting variance is 0.1111 (SD 0.3333). This follows by summing the squared weights λ²Σ(1−λ)^(2j), not by assuming steady state at startup.

The upward CUSUM recurrence C⁺[k] = max(0, C⁺[k−1]+z[k]−κ) yields (0.15, 0.50, 0.45, 1.00); the downward statistic stays zero. None crosses h = 1.5, and the largest raw z = 0.8 also stays inside Shewhart ±3. This four-point no-alarm trace is not a performance estimate. ARL is an expected number of points to signal under a fully specified in-control or shifted model; a finite-horizon alarm proportion is a different quantity. λ, L, κ, and h require separate calibration: choosing the same numerical multiplier does not equalize chart ARLs or finite-horizon false-alarm probabilities.

Assumptions and limitations

Standard designs often assume a stable baseline, known or well-estimated parameters, a specified shift form, and independent observations. Fab data can contain autocorrelation, tool changes, product mix, measurement delay, and gradual drift. Such changes can distort nominal ARL and make alerts cluster. Multivariate signals create additional multiplicity and dependence. Report the design assumptions, in-control false-alarm objective, detection scenarios, sampling interval, and reset rules. Control limits remain estimates of process behavior, not specifications for product acceptance.

Review questions

  1. Why is a Shewhart chart often responsive to large shifts but less sensitive to small persistent ones?
  2. What does reducing EWMA λ change, and what trade-off follows?
  3. For an intended 0.5σ shift, why might κ = 0.25 be a reasonable CUSUM design starting point?
  4. Why is ARL measured in points rather than hours, and when can it be converted?
